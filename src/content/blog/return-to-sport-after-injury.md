"When can I play again?" is the question every injured athlete asks. A safe return to sport after injury depends on more than time. The answer depends on the injury, but the path back looks much the same every time. At Revibe we break it into four stages so you always know where you are and what comes next.

## Stage 1: Restore

**Goal:** calm the pain, protect what's healing and keep moving.

This is the first few days to two weeks. We figure out exactly what's wrong, treat the pain and swelling, and give you a short plan you can do at home. Complete rest is rarely the answer. Gentle movement in a comfortable range usually helps healing more than doing nothing.

**You're ready to move on when** daily tasks like walking, sleeping and getting dressed are getting easier, and the pain is settling rather than building.

## Stage 2: Rebuild

**Goal:** get back the strength and movement you lost.

Injured tissue gets weaker quickly, and so do the muscles around it. In this stage the work becomes progressive strength training, built up session by session. For a knee that means squats, step-ups and single-leg work. For a shoulder it means rotator cuff and shoulder blade strength. For a back, hinging and carrying.

**You're ready to move on when** you have most of your strength back compared with the other side, and normal movements don't cause the pain to flare.

## Stage 3: Retrain

**Goal:** prepare the body for the speed and forces of your sport.

This is the stage people skip. Strength in the gym doesn't automatically mean you can sprint, cut, jump, land or throw at full speed. In Retrain we add those movements back in steps: running, hopping, landing, change of direction, throwing or overhead work, then sport-specific drills.

**You're ready to move on when** you can do your sport's key movements at full speed without pain, and testing shows both sides are close.

## Stage 4: Perform

**Goal:** return to full sport and stay there.

Before you go back to games, we test. Depending on the injury that can include force plate jumps, hop tests, strength testing and timed drills. If the injured side is still clearly behind, you're not finished, even if it feels fine.

Perform doesn't end when you're cleared. The goal is to keep you playing, so many athletes move into [performance training](/performance-training/) to keep building strength and reduce the chance of the same injury happening again.

## How long each stage takes

Every injury moves at its own speed. These are typical planning ranges our physiotherapy team uses:

| Injury | Typical return | When Perform usually starts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Mild to moderate ankle sprain](/sprained-ankle-recovery-time/) | 1 to 6 weeks to return to sport | Week 6 onward |
| [Kneecap pain or knee arthritis](/where-does-it-hurt/knee/) | 6 to 12 weeks of structured care | Week 12 onward |
| [Recent low back pain](/low-back-pain-recovery-time/) | 6 to 12 weeks of structured care | Week 12 onward |
| [Shoulder pain without surgery](/where-does-it-hurt/shoulder/) | Review at 8 weeks, often 12 weeks or more | Weeks 6 to 12 and on |
| [Tennis elbow, recent](/where-does-it-hurt/elbow-wrist/) | 8 to 12 weeks of structured care | Weeks 12 to 16 onward |

[After surgery](/pre-post-surgical-rehabilitation/), your surgeon's protocol sets the timeline and the restrictions, and we work within it. Concussion follows its own medical pathway and needs a doctor's guidance.

## Why rushing back backfires

Pain often settles well before the injured area is strong enough for sport. That's the trap. Athletes feel fine walking around, play a game, and re-injure it because the tissue and the muscles around it weren't ready for the speed and load.

A re-injury usually takes longer to recover from than the first one. Spending two or three more weeks in Retrain is almost always quicker than starting over.
