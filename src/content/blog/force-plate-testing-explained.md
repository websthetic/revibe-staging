Two athletes can jump the same height and get there in completely different ways. One sinks low and grinds up. The other dips fast and springs off the floor. The tape measure says they're equal. A force plate shows they need different training.

That's the reason we test before we train.

## What a force plate is

A force plate is a flat platform that measures the force you put into it, about a thousand times a second. Stand on it and it reads your body weight. Jump on it and it records every part of the movement: the dip, the turnaround at the bottom, the push off the floor and the landing.

At Revibe we use a pair of plates, one under each foot. That's what lets us compare your left and right side.

## The test: five jumps

The main test is the countermovement jump test. You stand tall, dip down quickly and jump as high as you can, hands on your hips. We take five good reps. Five reps gives us a reliable average instead of one lucky jump.

It takes a few minutes. Nothing about it is hard on the body, which is why we can use it on athletes [coming back from injury](/return-to-sport-after-injury/) too.

Depending on your goals, the [baseline test](/start-the-year-with-a-baseline-test/) can also include timed sprints and strength testing.

## What the numbers mean

From those jumps we group the results into four categories. Each one points to a different kind of training.

**Braking.** How well you absorb force on the way down. This is the same quality you use to stop, cut and land. Low braking numbers often show up in athletes who struggle to change direction or keep tweaking [knees](/where-does-it-hurt/knee/) and [ankles](/sprained-ankle-recovery-time/).

**Force.** How hard you drive into the ground on the way up. This leans on raw strength. If force is your weak spot, the answer is usually heavier strength work.

**Elasticity.** How quickly you turn the dip into the jump. Some people are strong but slow to use it. Their training needs more speed and reactive work, not just more weight on the bar.

**Power.** The result: jump height and takeoff speed. Power tells you where you are. The other three tell you why.

On top of the four categories, the plates show how each side contributes. If one leg is doing noticeably more of the work, that's worth knowing, especially after an injury.

## What you get back

You get a baseline report written for you, not a printout of raw data. It shows:

- Your scores in each category, compared with a D1 and pro benchmark
- Your strengths, so you know what not to waste time on
- Your development priorities, which become the focus of your training block
- Left and right side differences, where they matter

Your coach writes the program from those numbers and coaches every session, usually two to three times a week. At the end of the block we run the same test again, and you get a new report that shows what changed.
