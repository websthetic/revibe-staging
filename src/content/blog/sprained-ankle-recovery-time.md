A rolled ankle is one of the most common injuries we see at Revibe, from basketball and soccer players to people who stepped off a curb wrong. It's also one of the most under-treated. The swelling goes down, walking feels fine, and people go straight back to their sport. A few weeks later they roll it again.

Sprained ankle recovery time depends on the grade of the sprain and how well it's rehabbed. Here's how long a sprain actually takes, what each stage looks like, and what decides whether yours is on the short or long end.

## What a sprain is

An ankle sprain is a stretch or tear of the ligaments that hold the ankle joint together. The most common is a lateral sprain, where the foot rolls inward and the ligaments on the outside of the ankle take the load.

Sprains are usually graded by how much of the ligament is damaged:

- **Mild:** stretched, with some tenderness and swelling. You can walk on it.
- **Moderate:** partly torn. More swelling and bruising, and walking hurts.
- **Severe:** fully torn. Large swelling, the ankle may feel loose, and putting weight on it is hard.

The grade gives a rough idea of the timeline, but how you rehab it matters just as much.

## Recovery timeline, week by week

At Revibe we split recovery into four stages. They overlap, and you move on when the ankle passes each stage, not when the calendar says so.

| Stage | Typical weeks | What's happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Restore | Week 0 to 1 | Calm the pain and swelling, protect the ankle, keep it moving gently |
| Rebuild | Weeks 1 to 4 | Calf, foot and balance strength, walking without a limp |
| Retrain | Weeks 3 to 8 | Hopping, landing, running and change of direction |
| Perform | Week 6 onward | Back to full sport, with testing to check both sides match |

**Restore.** For the first few days, protect the ankle without locking it up. Gentle movement in a pain-free range helps more than complete rest. If walking is very painful, a brace or a short time on crutches may be needed.

**Rebuild.** Once the swelling settles, the work shifts to calf raises, foot strength and single-leg balance. Balance matters more than people think. The ligaments that tear in a sprain also help your brain sense where your foot is, and that sense needs retraining.

**Retrain.** This is where most people skip ahead. Walking and jogging in a straight line feel fine, so they go back to games. But cutting, landing from a jump and stopping quickly put far more load on the ankle. We build those back in steps: hops in place, then side to side, then landings, then sport drills. Our guide to [getting back to sport after an injury](/return-to-sport-after-injury/) walks through the same four stages for other injuries.

**Perform.** Before you go back to full sport, we test it. On our [force plates](/force-plate-testing-explained/), we compare how each leg absorbs and produces force on a jump. If your sprained side is still well behind the other, you're not done, even if it feels fine. Once you're cleared, [performance training](/performance-training/) keeps the ankle strong for the season ahead.

## What makes recovery longer

- **A previous sprain on the same ankle.** Repeat sprains tend to take longer, and each one raises the risk of the next.
- **Going back too early.** The ligament may be healing while strength and balance are still below where they need to be.
- **No rehab at all.** Many people with a sprain never do the balance and strength work. That's the main reason some ankles stay weak or keep giving way years later.
- **A more serious injury than a sprain.** A small fracture or damage to the joint surface can feel like a bad sprain at first.

## When to see a doctor or get an X-ray

See a doctor or go to urgent care if:

- You couldn't take four steps right after the injury, or still can't
- The bone on the inside or outside of the ankle, or along the outside edge of the foot, is tender when pressed
- The ankle looks deformed, or you have numbness in the foot
- Swelling and pain are getting worse after the first few days instead of better

Those signs can point to a fracture, which needs to be ruled out before rehab starts.
