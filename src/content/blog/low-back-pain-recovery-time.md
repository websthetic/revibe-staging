"How long does low back pain last?" is the first question almost everyone asks when they come in with back pain. It's a fair question. It's hard to plan work, training or a family holiday when you don't know if you'll be stuck for a week or a season.

Low back pain recovery time varies, but there are realistic ranges. Here's that range, what changes it, and what each stage of recovery looks like.

## The realistic timeline

Most back pain gets better. The pain itself often eases within a few weeks. Getting back to full strength, so it doesn't flare up again the next time you lift something awkward, takes longer.

| Type of back pain | Typical timeline |
| --- | --- |
| Recent low back pain | 6 to 12 weeks of structured care |
| Pain that has lasted or keeps coming back | 3 to 6 months |
| Sciatica (pain down the leg) | 8 to 16 weeks |
| Nerve symptoms (tingling, numbness) | Can take 3 to 6 months to settle |

These are planning ranges, not promises. Your assessment sets the timeline for your back.

If you want to know whether your pain is more likely muscular or coming from a disc, our earlier post on [muscular vs disc back pain](/lower-back-pain-muscular-or-disc/) goes into the differences.

## The four stages

At Revibe we split recovery into four stages. They overlap, and you move forward based on how your back responds, not just the date.

**Restore (weeks 0 to 2).** Calm things down. Hands-on treatment such as [massage therapy](/services/massage/) or [osteopathy](/services/osteopathy/), gentle movement and a few simple exercises you can do at home. The goal is to make getting out of bed, sitting in the car and walking easier.

**Rebuild (weeks 2 to 6).** Strength work for your trunk and hips, built up session by session. This is where people start to trust their back again.

**Retrain (weeks 6 to 12).** Lifting, carrying, hinging and rotating, plus whatever your work or sport demands. A long shift, a long drive, a full training session.

**Perform (week 12 onward).** Back to everything, with a plan to keep it strong. For some people that means a few check-ins. For others it means moving into [performance training](/performance-training/).

## What makes back pain take longer

- **How long you've had it.** Pain that has lasted more than three months usually takes longer to settle, partly because the body has become more sensitive to it.
- **Leg symptoms.** Sciatica and nerve symptoms take longer than pain that stays in the back.
- **Stopping all activity.** Resting completely for days often leads to more stiffness and a slower recovery. Moving within what you can tolerate tends to help.
- **Fear of movement.** If bending or lifting feels dangerous, people avoid it, and the back gets weaker. Part of our job is showing you what's safe.
- **Sleep and stress.** Both affect how much pain you feel and how quickly you recover. Long days at a desk play a part too; our post on [neck pain from desk work](/neck-pain-tension-headaches-desk-work/) covers the desk changes that help.

## Do you need a scan?

Usually not at first. Most low back pain doesn't need an X-ray or MRI to be treated well, and scans often show changes that are normal for your age and not the cause of your pain. A doctor may order one if the pain isn't improving with treatment or if there are signs of something more serious.

## When to see a doctor first

Go to emergency right away if you have back pain with any of these:

- Loss of control of your bladder or bowels
- Numbness in the groin or around the inner thighs
- Weakness in one or both legs that is getting worse

See your doctor soon if your back pain comes with fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, or followed a serious fall or accident.
